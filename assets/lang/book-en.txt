TITLE=Freedie of Skydysh

# The Town

The town lies along the sea in a long strip and ends in woods at either side. To the south is the promenade with its palms and low parapet, below the parapet sand and parasols, then the water, and on the water a spit and a tall green island with a lighthouse. The lighthouse is cream, with red bands; nobody goes up to its gallery.

On the promenade stands a big old house with a tower and an amber lamp, a square in front of it. To the west is the factory with its chimney, where they make glass for the street lamps. To the east is the warehouse, and past the warehouse the port: a yard behind a fence, stacks of containers and a crane over them.

West of the house is the old town. The houses there are low and warm in colour, the lanes so narrow a pickup will not go through, and the market has a clock tower. North of the old town is the concert hall, with columns and a gilded lyre over the door. On the main street, north of the house, is the town hall: pale stone, a clock over the door, a flag, and a pad with the letter H on the roof. The archive is far off, to the north-east, at the very edge: a low grey house with a garden behind it.

The town runs back from the sea a little over six hundred metres and ends in fields. Past the fields is a ridge, in the ridge a pass, beyond the pass a village and an old quarry. On the mountain to the north-west stands the tower of LAB Inc., and the road to it winds twice round the mountain. By the last street, on the road out of town, is a dark brick house with two gates and a turquoise plate above them. That is the brums' workshop.

People and brums live in the town. Brums are made of stone, the height of a person, and each has a plate with a number on its forehead. People sit on benches, lie on loungers, cross at the zebra and step out of the road when a pickup comes at them. The sun here is low and warm nearly all day.

# The Workshop

The stone for her was brought from the quarry beyond the pass, in the same pickup the workshop uses to fetch stone for everyone. It took three loads; one is enough for an ordinary brum. The stone was stacked by the far wall, between the racks of plates and the reels of wire, and for some days it simply lay there while the workshop mended others.

The order came from the town service. They needed someone who would clear a rockfall on the pass, pull out whatever was stuck, carry what others could not lift, and not break in doing it. The order said "emergency work" and gave a height: two and a half metres. The weight was left blank.

They put her together at night, when the gates were shut. Two brums held the stone, a third fitted the joints and knocked on them with a mallet until a joint stopped sounding hollow. By morning a body stood there, taller than any of them by two heads, with arms that reached to the knees.

They gave her no plate on the forehead. Where a brum has its number, she has a room: a desk, a tube screen, a clock, a row of keys and one red one. The same third brum fitted the room. How it fitted in there, nobody at the workshop asked.

In the morning the first line came up on the tube. What it said was not written down. Freedie turned her head toward the gates, and the brum standing by the gates opened them.

A maid's dress turned up in a trunk of other people's things; its black skirt and white apron sat on the red brick at once. They gave her the mallet they had built her with: a wooden handle and a head in iron hoops. The name on the order was written in English, Freedie, and the town at once began to say it its own way.

# Home

At first they gave her a flat in the old house on the promenade, the one with the tower and the amber lamp. Later a house of her own was built for her in a quiet district, to the size of the flat: two storeys, cream plaster, tall windows with turquoise shutters, a coral canopy over the door, and in front a yard behind a low fence. The green brum lives in the old flat now. The flat is made for her height: ceilings seven metres high, doors she goes through without stooping. A kitchen, a bedroom, a dressing room and a hall with a very large fireplace. The fireplace has never been lit. There are plants in pots on the sills, many windows, and in the evening you can see the sea from them.

From the yard there is a second door into the house, straight into the bedroom. Across the street from the front door is a shop, beside it a taxi rank, and to the east a park. A place was left on the roof where a helicopter could land; when she moved in, she had no helicopter.

In the hall stands a sofa. She sits on it when there is no work, and in the evening stretches her legs out as far as the fireplace. On the sofa she waits for the next line to come up on the tube, and the sofa remembers where she left off. She need not wait: she can get up, go out to the promenade and walk wherever she likes. The town takes nothing off for it.

By the kerb at the door, in time, her own pickup appeared. But that came after the glass, of which later.

# Work

Work comes to the room in her forehead. On the tube, line by line, the word "Dispatch" is typed, then what has happened and where to go. Who types it is not written on the screen. Dispatch does not ask how she is and does not praise. At the end of a job it puts the time and a line with a heart: how many likes from the town. Sometimes likes come with no job at all, if on the way she has mended something nobody asked her to.

Freedie is silent. Only the tube speaks, and only about work.

She has the mallet from the workshop and a screwdriver; they are never in her hands together, the other one hangs at her hip. She can throw the mallet, and the mallet comes back to her hand. In the dark she has a torch, and the light goes where she looks.

Her vehicle is the duty pickup. There are several in town, standing by the pavements, and any of them is hers. A duty pickup does not break. Cars move themselves out of the road after it, and the town puts the lamps back up. There is always a ladder in the back; for special work they add a rope or charges. Anything taken from the back goes back into it after the work.

# People She Knows

The green brum lives in her old flat, in the old house on the promenade. His brick is green where Freedie's is red, and his mallet is the same, only smaller. At home he sits on her old sofa just as she sits on hers, one leg stretched out. In the morning he goes out to his house and strikes the button by the door with his mallet. Why, he has never told anyone.

The pilot of the town helicopter is a brum of black brick. He flies the town office's papers over the roofs, because it is quicker than the traffic, and he does not like windy days.

In the town hall's secretariat, behind the door with the gold frame, works a clerk in a lilac cardigan. She holds folders out in front of her in both hands and hands them over the same way. In the archive, at the end of the corridor, the clerk on duty sits at a desk and takes folders from anyone who brings them.

At the workshop works a brum of grey slate. He drives to the quarry for stone and mends traffic lights. How he and Freedie met is in the fourth story.

In the concert hall a pianist plays in the evenings. He sits down at the piano and makes up a piece on the spot, out of old themes he remembers, and never plays the same thing twice. The hall has a caretaker; when there is no light, he is the one who calls.

The searcher brums of violet brick are neither her friends nor her enemies. They live off what they find and run over the same roofs she does.

# The First Story. Burning Plates

The morning began with the sea. The wind came across the beach and the square to the house with the tower, and the sun began to darken at one edge. While the moon came over it, one bright patch was left on the square. By the old house the green brum struck the button with his mallet, and everything went white. When the white cleared, Freedie was standing in the yard of the new house.

Five brums with burning foreheads were running through the streets. Their plates were fakes, from a cellar, cheap. For the first weeks such plates work no worse than real ones, then they overheat. A brum with a burning plate does not hear the street and cannot stop by itself.

On the tube it said: "Five brums, five minutes." Freedie ran.

She caught the first by a bench on the promenade and threw the mallet at the back of his head. The plate flew off, the stone scattered over the paving, the mallet came back to her hand. The second turned into a lane, the third went down to the beach between the parasols. People stepped out of the road and looked round. One lamp she knocked down with her shoulder.

On the fifth there was less than a minute left on the clock.

"The brums are already being put back together at the workshop," the tube wrote, and under it, once in her whole life: "Head home." She walked along the sea. Where the fakes came from, the service still did not know.

# The Second Story. The Folder on the Roofs

The town office helicopter was circling over the roofs. The wind had torn a folder out of its net, and it lay somewhere among the chimneys. The folder held estimates and drawings, without which a building site could not start.

Three violet brums reached the house under the helicopter before Freedie. They knew the finder's rule: whoever brings the folder back first is the one the office pays. One was already climbing the fire escape, the other two were going through the arch into the yard.

Freedie climbed the next ladder along. After that came roofs of different heights, with gaps over the yards between them. One gap she jumped, into another she fell and climbed out again. On the third roof a searcher came out to meet her. Freedie threw the mallet, and the searcher scattered by a chimney. They will put him back together at the workshop, like anyone.

The folder lay in a gutter at the very edge, red, tied with black tape. Freedie took it under her arm.

"Found. Take it to the town hall, to the secretariat."

In the secretariat the clerk in the lilac cardigan came out from behind her desk and held out her hands. Freedie held out the folder.

The folder is the clerk's now, in both hands in front of her. "Found, thank you. It goes to work at once."

Freedie went home across the square. The helicopter flew away.

# The Third Story. Glass, Containers and Light in the Hall

There are many lamps in town, and they fall often: some from the wind and lorries, others from the duty pickup. The glass for them is made at the factory west of the house and handed out from the warehouse to the east.

Freedie took the wheel for the first time. The tube said how to hold the wheel, how to give it gas and how to back up, and never came back to it. By the factory gate there was a yellow bay. She put the pickup into it at the second try and stood while four crates were loaded into the back. Glass does not like to be hurried, and along the promenade she drove slower than she could. At the warehouse the crates were taken off.

"The crates are at the warehouse," wrote the tube, and at once the next thing: "In the port, past the warehouse, a ship has been unloaded. The containers are standing anyhow."

The port was a hundred metres away. She did not need the pickup. By the fence stood a tower with a ladder, and at the top the crane's cabin. From the cabin the yard lay open like a hand: stacks, every colour mixed in each. The crane took the top one off and set it where she pointed: on an empty place or on its own colour, no higher than four. Twice she took a move back. When every stack held four of one colour, "Done" lit up on the panel.

The tube spoke up right there in the cabin. "Service call: the concert hall, north of the old town. The audience is in, and there is no light."

She came down the tower, got into the pickup by the warehouse and drove across the whole town. The wheel obeyed her now. The hall had columns, broad steps and the lyre over the door. Inside it was dark; you could only hear the people whispering in the rows. Freedie switched on her torch. The beam went over the backs of the seats, over the piano and over the pianist standing beside it. The door to the wings was to the right of the stage.

In the panel the wires lay in squares, and nearly all of them had come apart. She turned square after square, and the current from the feed in the middle went further and further. Three lamps came on before the rest, but there was still no light in the hall. Then they all came on at once.

The pianist sat down and began to play. Freedie found her seat and sat. She did not listen long. The hall grew dark, but not because of the panel.

In the morning she was sitting on her own sofa. How she had got home, the tube did not say. "Morning. Last night the hall had its light, and the concert went on." By the kerb under the windows stood a pickup that had not been there before.

# The Fourth Story. The Pass, the Quarry and the Traffic Light

Beyond the ridge is a village, and there is one road to it. Bread and post go along it, and the doctor's van. After rain the slope over the pass slides: logs, stumps, stones and clay jam between the rocks and stand there like a wall.

Out of town the pickup held the bends of the road by itself. Past the last bend the rockfall came into view, and the tube had time to say: "The rockfall is jammed. It needs a charge from the back." The pickup stopped as if at a wall. Freedie got out and struck it with the mallet. Dust came off, and nothing else.

In the back lay a ladder, a rope and charges. She took a charge, set it at the very foot of the rockfall and went back to the pickup. After the blast a heap was left, and that could be cleared with anything: the mallet, a shoulder, the pickup. In the village people came out onto the road and looked toward the pass.

The tube said the pass was open, and straight after: "In the quarry east of the road, a workshop brum's pickup is stuck."

The quarry was old, with gravel on the floor and ledges on three sides. On the floor stood a pickup in mud up to the axles, and beside it the brum of grey slate. He came here for stone, as once he had come for stone for her. Freedie tied the rope to his pickup, the other end to her own, and pulled. The mud did not let go at once. The brum's pickup could not drive any more, and it was sent to the workshop.

The brum had not come only for stone. A traffic light had broken in town, and he was the one meant to mend it. Through Dispatch he asked Freedie to take the job on.

At the crossing every post was blinking yellow. Cars went across slowly, people crossed where they liked. On the post to the right of the northbound traffic hung a grey box. Freedie hung the mallet at her hip and took the screwdriver. Inside were the ends of the wires of three lamps, red, yellow and green. She joined each pair with a wire of its colour, and the posts lit up in turn, as they should.

Since then the traffic lights in town sometimes break again. If Freedie passes and mends one, the town sends likes. She got home that day well after sunset.

# The Fifth Story. The Puppy, the Archive and the Landing Gear

People had gathered on the beach past the warehouse. Some shuffled at the water's edge, others walked along the shore and back again, six were calling out with their hands cupped round their mouths. Three had waded in to the knee and gone no further. Far from the shore a puppy was keeping itself up in the water. It was no longer swimming toward the shore.

A brum is heavier than water, but it swims. Freedie jumped down from the promenade, went between the people and walked into the sea. She took the puppy in her palm and swam back keeping the palm above the water.

The vets' clinic stands on the third street from the sea: a white house with a turquoise stripe and a paw over the door. Freedie put the pickup in the yellow bay, got out with the puppy and went to the door. The glass doors slid apart and closed behind her.

She came out of the clinic without the puppy. There was new work on the tube already: "The secretariat has a folder of documents ready. The archive expects it today."

The town hall's lobby was crowded. The clerk in the lilac cardigan was waiting at the secretariat door with a red folder in her hands, held just as she had held the one from the roofs. "Documents for the archive. Carefully. The helicopter is waiting on the roof."

The folder is Freedie's now. The lift was a tight fit for her. On the roof, on the pad with the letter H, stood the helicopter, the black pilot in the cabin. Freedie sat down beside him, and he flew in an arc over the town, across the whole town, to the garden behind the archive.

The helicopter set down on the round pad. The right landing strut broke, flew onto the lawn, and it lay over on its right side. The pilot stayed in his seat. Freedie climbed out with the folder and went into the archive, down the corridor past the row of offices, to the duty clerk's desk. The folder is his now.

At the archive door the tube wrote: "Nobody takes off on one strut. There is a new one at the brums' workshop." A duty pickup stood by the pavement. In front of the workshop gates, the very ones she had once come out of, a strut was laid in the back. On the way back she carried the strut across her chest, set it by the helicopter's door and knocked it home with the mallet. The helicopter straightened.

Freedie stepped three metres back from the door, out past the circle of the blades, and looked at it. Then she sat down at the controls. The pilot sat beside her.

"You are at the controls. Two rings under your thumbs," said the tube, and then about the left and the right. She lifted the helicopter over the garden, over the archive's roof, and flew it to the town hall. She did not land on the H pad the first time.

The pilot got out onto the roof and went to the lift. The helicopter was left to her.

# The Sixth Story. The Navigator

On the mountain north-west of town stands the tower of LAB Inc. There they have made a navigator for pickups: a map in the corner of the screen, a blue line on the map. Freedie drove up in her own pickup, under the arch and twice round the mountain. In the lobby a brum stood behind the counter with a box. The box is Freedie's now.

By the pickup she unpacked it, and the navigator settled by the wheel. For five minutes she drove along the line, address after address: the quarry, the vets' clinic, the concert hall, the old town. She knew all those places without the line. The clock ran only while she drove along the line; whenever she turned the wrong way, it waited. Afterwards the navigator stayed in the pickup.

# The Seventh Story. Hotheads

At night LAB Inc. tried out its collider. A black hole the size of a cherry was born in the chamber, and from its pole a thin violet beam struck straight across the night shift of brums on their chargers. Fifteen brums glowed red to the core, and in each chest something ticked. Of all their programming, one thing was left: to follow the nearest working brum.

In the morning they came down into the town and stood in threes where they had once been set up. One had picked up a golden mallet at the workshop. There turned out to be one working brum in town, and that was Freedie. Had they touched her, the burnt firmware would not have held.

She went round the groups in turn; each one saw her and followed. By the beach a whole crowd was trailing after her. Along the spit she led them onto the island, up the winding path, to the lighthouse. She herself left the island by sea, on a yacht from the jetty. The bridge on the spit was raised.

# The Eighth Story. Black Cherry

The hole stayed in its trap over the detector, the size of a cherry. The ring of magnets that kept it quiet had gone out of true: six magnets had been torn from their posts. The hole spun, and its beam went round the hall like a lighthouse beam. The LAB brums could not go down there.

Freedie went down in the lift from the lobby. The torn magnets blinked red. She went to each one and struck it with the mallet, and the magnet went back in place and lit up green. With each one the beam went slower. By the trap it ran high, near the walls it came down to the floor, and there she jumped over it. In the middle she had to get out of its way.

When the sixth magnet turned green, the beam stopped. The hole hung over the detector and no longer spun. Freedie went up to the lobby, out to the pickup and drove down, twice round the mountain, home. In the evening she sat on the sofa and stretched her legs out to the fireplace. The tube was empty.
